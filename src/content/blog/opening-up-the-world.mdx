85 days, 8 countries, and I was almost never alone.

I do it solo, or I never do it. This is my rationale for putting myself in uncomfortable situations in the hunt for adventure. Solo travel is simply the only way I've found to do exactly what I want, and I almost always end up doing it with people who want the same thing.

I'm not a travel writer, and this definitely isn't a guide. It's an opinion, pieced together from notes I captured on my phone for about 20 seconds at 8:30pm each day. On previous trips I only wrote down what I did, and found that I never revisited those notes, so this time I wrote down what I thought instead. Only a handful of people I met on the road were happy to discuss the psychology of solo travelling with me, so, like the last post, I'm putting the jigsaw together on paper and tying all my notes into one piece of writing.

In the last post I wrote about four lines from my notes in Bukhara; *Time flies. You don't have long. And you can only rely on yourself. Be your own best friend.* In this post I'd like to come back to those lines, the last one in particular, now that this trip is ending.

My opinion, for what it's worth, is that solo travel opens up the world. You see new places, of course, however the bigger change is realising how much more is possible than you were ever taught.

There were two people I met in particular who helped get the ball rolling, and showed me a flaw in my perspective of the world.

## Trained to be scared.

A woman I met in Georgia, also travelling on her own, said something I haven't been able to shake; "I think we're trained to be scared of the world." Not long after, I read something making the same point about my generation. Don't talk to strangers, don't go there, don't do that. Each warning makes sense on its own, but once you stack them all together you don't do anything. As a result, we stay in our bubble, and we call it being sensible.

Hitchhiking is the perfect example. I'd always assumed it was dangerous, because that's what I was taught. I'm writing this from Dahab, where almost everyone has come to dive, and just up the coast at the Blue Hole there's a memorial to the divers who never came back up, a stark reminder of how risky diving can be. Nobody here thinks twice about it, yet when I tell people I've been hitchhiking, they're shocked. It can be argued that we've simply been taught which risks are acceptable, and hitchhiking didn't make the list.

So who wrote the list? I'd say nobody in particular. Fear is easy to teach; a warning costs nothing to give, and nobody is ever blamed for one. If you tell someone not to hitchhike and they never do, no one will ever know what they missed. One bad story also travels a lot further than a thousand uneventful rides, and there's no film about the car that stopped and dropped you at the next town. The algorithm hasn't helped either, as a scary story keeps us watching for longer. Pass all of that down a generation or two and the warnings start to sound like common sense. From what I've seen, the world is a lot less scary than we were taught, and humans are humans wherever you go.

I should be clear about what I'm not saying. I don't want to tell anyone how to travel, which risks to take, or what you need to do to reach the same realisations I have. I'm also a white man travelling alone, and I know that makes parts of this easier for me than they are for plenty of others. However, opening up the world doesn't require a thumb on the side of a highway. If your comfort level is a tour, take the tour; personally, I take plenty of them. We accept a level of discomfort to explore the world and create memories, but discomfort doesn't need to be risk.

What surprised me most was how capable the rest of the world is. A friend on the road hurt her back, and three days later she was still in pain. I suggested a physio, and her answer was "there are physios here?" Of course there are, we were in a capital city. If you forget a pair of jocks or your toothbrush, there's a shop down the road. You're the one travelling, but you'll be surprised how similar the rest of the world is, and how much of it already works.

## Seeing is believing.

There's a line in my notes I keep coming back to; *it's all half known, because you hear these things but don't digest them until you come to the realisation on your own.* Everyone has been told the world is bigger than their routine, however very few of us act on it, and I'd argue that's because hearing about it is rarely enough. You need to see someone actually do it.

That's what the second of those two people did for me. About a week into this trip, I met a solo female traveller who had hitchhiked from London to Kazakhstan via Russia while only couch surfing. We finished a hike together with no reception and no public transport, so she walked up to the road, stuck her thumb out, and the very first car took her… leaving me behind, as I'd said I'd wait for a taxi. This was the moment my mentality shifted; if she was brave enough to try, so was I. I copied her steps, smiled, threw out my thumb, and the third car picked me up.

I later wrote that it *got my mind thinking about possibilities and shifted my perspective of how we treat the world.* I'd heard of people doing this before, but watching someone do it made it real.

Sticking your thumb out on the side of the road seems like a small act, however when a car stops it changes a lot more than how you get to the next town; strangers stop for strangers, you can go somewhere without a plan, and the world turns out to be a lot kinder than you were told. At the time I wrote about *the possibilities this creates in life, you gain awareness of the kindness of strangers… I can just stick out my thumb and hopefully go.*

What I didn't expect was how far one experience spreads. There was a hike everyone told us not to do, which we'd ruled out ourselves, and we did it anyway. Afterwards I wrote that it *made everything seem possible*, and I meant everything, well beyond the next hike or the next country. At the start of this trip, hitchhiking and turning up to a remote guest house without a booking scared me, and by the end I didn't want to do it any other way.

I've travelled solo before and loved it. Looking back, my last solo trip through China was more of a physical adventure than a mental one, as the language barrier meant I barely spoke to anyone until I reached the international cities. This time I was with someone almost every day, and that's the difference; the places gave me the physical adventure, and the people I met gave me the mental one, because they were the ones showing me what was possible.

## One person to organise.

Of course, seeing what's possible is only useful if you're free to act on it, which is the practical reason I travel solo. Solo means there's one of you to organise, which is naturally more flexible than six. You don't have to worry about what six other people want, or debate where to eat and what to do. You choose independently, you follow your own desires, you do things without judgement, and you can be whoever you want to be at any time.

It took me a while to see how much is hiding in that. Every group decision is a small negotiation, and if you've ever travelled in a group you'll know your suggestions start to bend towards whatever you think everyone will agree to. Do that for long enough and you lose track of what you wanted in the first place. Travelling solo takes that negotiation away.

The bigger cost of travelling as a group of six is all the possibilities nobody suggests because they'd be too hard to sell; the lift from a stranger, the extra day in a valley, the detour someone mentioned over breakfast. Solo, all it takes is one person being curious. I wouldn't have gone to Jordan this year if I'd waited for friends. Time flies, you don't have long, and waiting for everyone to be free is how most of these possibilities quietly disappear.

When I say without judgement, I'd argue any audience, friends included, makes you a little more like the person they expect you to be. On your own, you can be chatty at breakfast and silent by dinner, and nobody needs an explanation. Pick the right hostel and you can have a chat over coffee, or ignore the world and do your own thing. Company is optional every hour of the day, which almost never happens at home; freedom to choose, and freedom to choose who with. I'm not saying don't travel with friends, it's always good, it's just not quite the same.

Then there are the decisions. Where to next, where to sleep, what to eat; solo, every decision is yours, and most are unknowns. Slowly you realise you can't care about every minor detail, or beat yourself up for choosing one over the other. You won't always choose right, and it will matter which one you choose, but it works out, and every time it does, it proves to you that you can do it. Once you realise how competent you've become, you gain a sense of calm in situations most people at home would gasp at.

## Almost never alone.

Choosing everything yourself still leaves plenty of room for company. Solo travel, at least the way I do it, is about embracing the spontaneous side of life and ending up doing something you'd never have foreseen, usually with people you'd never have met. My notes put it more bluntly; *just talk to people. So many people are here solo. So many people don't want to be solo. They don't travel to be isolated.*

The people at a hostel are solo travellers, same as you, there to do the things they want to do with other people who have reached the same conclusion, which is why friendships form so easily. The hardest part of meeting someone at home is finding out whether they want what you want; on the road, the fact they're there has already answered it.

Travelling this way made me realise how often we settle for convenience friendships at home. It's far easier to hang out with people who do the same things and live in the same area, and to meet at the pub each weekend because that's the common ground where everyone can catch up at once. Hostels work in a similar way, except every change of location, or of the people around you, creates new common ground, and new people to share it with.

In the last post I wrote about algorithms deciding what's important for us, and the same algorithms decide who we see online; people our age, liking the same things. A hostel table is about as far from that as you can get, as the only filter is that everyone chose to be there. It will sit you next to people you'd never meet at home, and you can join them on an adventure, have a laugh, try something new and learn so much from them. In a week you can live a dozen other ways of life, something the algorithm can only ever show you on a screen.

After a slightly embarrassing moment for one of our group, a friend I made in Dahab said this; "never bring an idiot with you when you travel, because you'll find one when you get there." It's a joke, but there's something true underneath it; anything you can find at home, you can find elsewhere, company included, and when you've had enough of one group, you move on to the next.

Unfortunately, moving on means sorting through people all over again. The only way to find the ones you click with is to get through the small talk first; where are you from, how long have you been travelling, where are you heading next. It takes effort, especially when you're sick or tired, and it's one of those discomforts you learn to accept. In my experience, the sorting is the only part that's shallow. Once you find your people, you can spend 16 hours a day together for a few weeks and develop a bond extremely quickly. Three weeks of that is 336 hours, which is more time than two years of catching up at the pub for three hours every weekend. Sometimes you just click, and they'll be a friend for life.

Starting from zero is a skill most adults stop practising once work, partners and old friends cover their social life by default, and solo travel resets you to zero every few days. Generally, being more social makes you happier, even when it's with strangers. After two months I found myself greeting people as I walked into a cafe, or politely waving to people on the street, and I'm sure it didn't do them any harm either.

Which brings me back to being your own best friend. If there's one skill to solo travel, I'd argue it's that; being good enough company for yourself that you never have to settle for bad company. The test is simple. What would you do with your best friend if they were here with you right now? Go and do it by yourself, and see if you enjoy it.

Being by yourself is a skill you need to train. The vast majority of people who have turned their nose up at solo travel don't realise this, and a lot of the negative things I hear come down to people's inability to be by themselves, or a few bad hostels, and both are understandable. It's extremely tough to drop a routine, fly somewhere entirely different and expect to last six months with no training; solo travelling can be extremely overwhelming, extremely often, and it's better not to go in unprepared. Some people are more gifted at it, same as learning a language, however everyone can train it; you learn to pick the right hostels, you learn to be on your own, and if you need to, you can fake it until you make it.

Those with experience know the downsides, and they're rarely discussed with people trying it for the first time; the mental demand of choosing everything every day, the odd moment of loneliness, food poisoning in a place where you have no friends or family. It's tough, so the training starts at home, in small batches, and almost all of it comes down to one lesson; learning to live with discomfort.

## Discomfort creep.

You can't avoid discomfort, and the discomfort is part of the point. Halfway through this trip, I wrote that *I think I am extremely comfortable in discomfort right now.* The more discomfort I sat in, the more comfortable I became, until I'd ended up in a comfort zone of the uncomfortable.

Most of it is everyday stuff; avoiding scams, waiting hours at a train station, bread and jam without a coffee for breakfast four days in a row. None of it is dangerous, and I've come to think of it as maintenance. We all know lifestyle creep; your comforts rise with your income, until things you used to be fine with become things you can't do without. Discomfort creep is the reverse, the more you can accept, the more of the world opens up to you. I'd argue most people can afford the flight, and it's the bunk bed they can't tolerate that closes the door.

*PSA: If you avoid hostels, you're not allowed to discard the rest of this post. There are plenty of other ways to meet people; stay in a hotel and invite yourself in for a drink at a hostel with a bar, ask a hostel receptionist whether there are any group tours you can join, or try the app NomadTable. All of these work.*

The bigger shift is acceptance. I wrote this on a trek; *accepting you're going to do something, and accepting things will work out, makes a world of difference…* Accepting discomfort has given me a far more peaceful mentality about doing new things. When I say everything will work out, I mean I'm now confident I'll handle whatever happens, and once every outcome is acceptable, saying yes becomes very easy.

That said, a little bit of fear every now and then is healthy. Try diving, or ride on the back of a Grab motorbike taxi (wear a helmet), and a new experience of danger has a way of reminding you that the daily mundane shouldn't be of great concern.

Calm is what all of this looks like from the outside, and other people tend to notice it before you do. At the Uzbekistan land border crossing, a fellow traveller praised me for staying level-headed through all of it; the pushing in the lines, the taxi drivers harassing you, the heat, the exhaustion of being awake all night on the bus. It made me acknowledge the skills I'd developed. Being calm in discomfort is a sign of confidence in your own ability, and it's the kind of self-belief that's hard to work on at home in routine. That's a post for another day.

## Everything is learning.

Not every day of this trip was enjoyable; some were exhausting, some frustrating, and a few I'd happily forget. "Travel isn't always pretty. It isn't always comfortable. Sometimes it hurts, it even breaks your heart. But that's okay. The journey changes you; it should change you. It leaves marks on your memory, on your consciousness, on your heart, and on your body. You take something with you. Hopefully, you leave something good behind." - Anthony Bourdain.

Many of the stories I'll retell for years were uncomfortable at the time, and I'd tell others not to repeat them, yet every one of them taught me something.

For me, this is what ties it all together. You go out and try things, the adventure gives you fulfilment, the fulfilment gives you contentment, and eventually the contentment gives you happiness. In short, you find purpose by going out and doing things. It's an old idea; John Stuart Mill wrote, "Ask yourself whether you are happy, and you cease to be so." Happiness seems to arrive while you're busy aiming at something else.

It can be argued that comfort gets you to contentment too, and much faster. Except, the issue is that contentment only lasts as long as the comfort does; it disappears as soon as you check out and head to the airport, whereas the contentment from an adventure stays with you long after you've come home.

I think we also overcomplicate the difficulties of enjoying the simple things, and rarely stop to appreciate them. Some of the best days I've had cost next to nothing; starting a fire to make a pot of tea with people worth talking to, a hike, the sunset, a swim, a night sleeping under the stars. Most of these you can do at home with what you already have, and nobody is selling them to us, so nobody is reminding us they exist.

We forget that the best things in life are free. Of course nothing is completely free, you still need to get there and eat, but you'd budget for that anyway, and creating memories doesn't need to cost any more than an ordinary day.

The best part is that it compounds. Every time you see someone do something you thought wasn't possible, you're more likely to try it yourself, and every time you try something, the next thing becomes a little easier to say yes to. Sooner or later you become the proof that the world isn't as scary as we were taught; the person at the hostel table who did the thing somebody else is scared of.

---

## I opened up.

So, did the world open up? I'd argue it was always this open; the capital always had physios, strangers were always willing to stop, and humans were always humans. Looking back, the thing that opened up was me.

Those four lines from Bukhara read differently now. *Time flies. You don't have long*; so don't wait for six people to be free. *You can only rely on yourself*; to go, at least, because the company turns up once you're there. *Be your own best friend*; so that company becomes something you choose. Somewhere in the middle of all this, I wrote that *I feel like I now understand 'solo' and 'travel' and how they fit together… It's all the highlights of spending time with friends but a lifestyle of it.* We're social creatures, and in routine we find ourselves lonelier than we'd like to admit. It's being called a loneliness epidemic, but I see travel as a saving grace.

If you've travelled this way, I'd genuinely like to know whether any of this rings true. If you haven't, maybe this is the proof you've been waiting for. Mine came from a stranger at a hostel table.

Consider this my thumb, stuck out on the side of the road.
